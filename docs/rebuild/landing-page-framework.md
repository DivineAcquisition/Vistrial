# Landing page framework

Date: 2026-11-10. Supplied by the owner. This is the framework Vistrial adopts for every landing page it
writes, lays out or reviews. Creative Production builds pages to it; landing page teardowns score pages
against it.

The core idea: a landing page is a sequence of objections answered in the order the reader raises them.
Each section has one job, and the copy and layout serve only that job.

## Sections, in order

### 1. Nav

- **Job:** keep the next step always one tap away.
- **Copy:** logo and a CTA with a verb ("Book a call"), never "Submit" or "Learn more."
- **Layout:** fixed on scroll, minimal, no menu links. Extra links give the reader exits, and this page
  has one goal.

### 2. Hero

- **Job:** in 5 seconds, tell the right person "this is for you" and the wrong person "this isn't."
- **Copy:** three layers.
  - The qualifier badge names the avatar and a threshold ("Running an online business doing $10K+/mo?").
    It works as a filter, so anyone below the line leaves and everyone who stays has budget.
  - The headline states the outcome in the reader's words, not the mechanism.
  - The subline says how, in one sentence.
- **Scarcity note:** only if it's true (builds per month). Fake scarcity is easy to spot and kills trust.
- **Layout:** centered, large type, one CTA, plenty of whitespace. Proof metrics go below the CTA as a
  quiet strip. Skip them if they aren't real.

### 3. Results

- **Job:** prove it works before you explain how.
- **Copy:** lead each card with the number (the metric), then one line of context, then a direct quote
  with name and role. The number carries it and the quote makes it human.
- **Layout:** 3 cards in a row, with the metric as the largest element on each.
- **Rule:** no proof, no section. Placeholders hurt more than a gap.

### 4. Problem agitation

- **Job:** make the reader feel understood, then feel the cost of waiting.
- **Copy:** each pain is a specific, recognizable moment ("A lead texts at 9pm and nobody replies until
  morning"), followed by a cost tag ("~$X lost per month"). Specific beats general: vague pain gets nods,
  specific pain gets belief.
- **Layout:** a grid of 3 to 6 cards. Keep each to 2 lines. The cost tag sits on its own line in an
  accent color.

### 5. Solution

- **Job:** resolve every pain, one for one.
- **Copy:** reframe each problem as an outcome ("Every lead gets a reply in under 60 seconds"). Present
  tense, outcome first, no feature jargon.
- **Layout:** mirror the problem grid exactly, so the reader's eye connects each pain to its fix. An
  unmatched pain or fix breaks the logic.

### 6. The offer

- **Job:** show exactly what they get so the price feels small next to it.
- **Copy:** list every deliverable as a noun phrase. Then one line that removes effort ("You provide
  access. We build everything else."). Then price and delivery window. The effort line matters because
  the buyer's real fear is the work of implementing.
- **Layout:** a boxed or bordered container, visually distinct from everything else. This is the center
  of the page.

### 7. With vs without

- **Job:** make the status quo look like a choice with a cost.
- **Copy:** matched pairs, same topic on both sides, short phrases. The "without" side is concrete and a
  little painful, and the "with" side is the specific fix.
- **Layout:** two columns, with red and green or muted and bright. Equal row counts.

### 8. Process timeline

- **Job:** kill the fear of a long, messy project.
- **Copy:** 3 to 5 steps. Each ends with "Your time: 15 minutes" (or similar). The smaller the client's
  share, the easier the yes.
- **Layout:** a vertical or horizontal numbered line, with the time cost visually separate from the step
  description.

### 9. Your role vs ours

- **Job:** reinforce that it's low effort for them.
- **Copy:** the client's list is short (access, one call, approval) and ours is long.
- **Layout:** two unequal columns. The imbalance is the message.

### 10. FAQ

- **Job:** handle the objections that remain, so nobody leaves to "think about it."
- **Copy:** write the question in the buyer's voice, the way they'd actually say it. Required topics:
  price, guarantee, who it's for, how long, what if it doesn't work.
- **Layout:** an accordion, with the most price-related question near the top.

### 11. Final CTA

- **Job:** catch the reader who scrolled the whole page and is still deciding.
- **Copy:** one line that restates the outcome, one button, one scarcity or timing reminder.
- **Layout:** the same visual weight as the hero, so the page ends the way it began.

## Cross-page rules

- One page, one goal, one CTA type.
- Repeat the CTA at the nav, hero, offer, and final sections.
- Short lines, with each sentence doing one job. If a line can be cut without losing meaning, cut it.
- Vistrial should refuse to publish if any required section is empty or if the pain and solution counts
  don't match.

## Traffic temperature

The order above assumes a cold paid-ads visitor who doesn't know the user yet. For warm traffic
(referrals, email), Results can move up to right after the hero and Problem can shrink.

## How Vistrial uses this

- **Building a page (Creative Production):** every landing page follows these sections in this order,
  each with its one job. Results is left out when there is no real proof. Publishing is refused when a
  required section is empty or the pain and solution counts differ.
- **Reviewing a page (landing page teardown):** each section of a crawled page is matched to one of
  these jobs, so a report can say which objections a page answers, in what order, and which it skips.
  Across many pages in one industry, that shows which patterns the pages that are working share.
